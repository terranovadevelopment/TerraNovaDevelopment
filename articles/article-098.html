Medicare for All and the Myth of “Job Loss”

Why Eliminating Useless Work Is Not a Harm but a Healing

One of the most common arguments against Medicare for All is the claim that it would “destroy two million jobs.” That number sounds frightening until you look closely at what those jobs are, why they exist, and what they actually produce. When you do, the argument collapses. Because the truth is simple: most of the jobs that would disappear are jobs created by a broken system — jobs that count as GDP but produce almost nothing of real value for human health.

The Jobs That Exist Only Because the System Is Broken

The two million jobs in question are not doctors, nurses, EMTs, or caregivers. They are not the people who heal, comfort, or save lives. They are overwhelmingly jobs in:

  • private insurance companies
  • billing departments
  • claims processing
  • prior-authorization offices
  • coding and reimbursement teams
  • marketing and sales divisions
  • layers of administrative management

These jobs exist for one reason: our health-care system is a maze. Every insurer has its own rules, networks, prices, codes, and denial processes. Every hospital has to hire armies of staff just to fight with insurers for payment. Every clinic has to employ people whose entire job is to translate medical care into billing codes and chase down reimbursements.

This is not productive work. It is defensive work — work created to survive a dysfunctional system.

GDP Is Not the Same as Value

Economists count these jobs as part of GDP because they involve salaries, office buildings, software contracts, and corporate activity. But GDP doesn’t measure whether the work is useful. It only measures whether money moves.

By that logic, if every hospital hired 10,000 people to fill out forms all day, GDP would go up — even though patient care would not improve at all.

Medicare for All threatens these jobs precisely because it eliminates the waste that created them. It simplifies billing, removes private insurers, and replaces dozens of competing bureaucracies with one unified system. The result is fewer administrative workers — and more resources freed for actual care.

Eliminating Waste Is Not a Harm — It’s a Benefit

If a factory stops producing useless parts, we don’t mourn the loss of inefficiency. We celebrate the improvement. The same is true here.

The jobs that disappear under Medicare for All are jobs that:

  • do not heal anyone
  • do not improve outcomes
  • do not reduce suffering
  • do not expand access
  • do not make the system more humane

They exist because the system is fragmented, not because they are inherently valuable.

Medicare for All doesn’t eliminate meaningful work. It eliminates paperwork work — the kind that drains money, time, and energy away from patients.

Real Value Comes From Care, Not Bureaucracy

When everyone has coverage, more people seek care. That means:

  • more nurses
  • more medical assistants
  • more home-health workers
  • more mental-health professionals
  • more primary-care staff
  • more public-health workers
  • more community-based caregivers

These are jobs that produce real value — healthier people, stronger families, and more stable communities.

The shift is not from “employment” to “unemployment.” It is from bureaucratic employment to care-centered employment.

A Transition Is Needed — But It’s Not a Crisis

Any major reform requires a transition plan for displaced workers. Medicare for All proposals already include funding for retraining, job placement, and wage support. And many administrative workers have skills that translate easily into public-sector roles or health-care delivery.

The real crisis is the current system — where millions of people go without care while billions of dollars are spent on paperwork.

The Bottom Line

The “two million jobs lost” argument is a distraction. It counts bureaucratic waste as if it were productive labor. It treats paperwork as if it were health care. It confuses GDP with value.

Medicare for All eliminates jobs that should never have existed in the first place — jobs created by a system that forces hospitals and patients to fight insurers instead of focusing on healing.

What Medicare for All actually does is redirect resources toward the people who need care and the workers who provide it. It replaces waste with value, confusion with clarity, and fear with stability.
