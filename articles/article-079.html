What Happens to the Part B Premium Under Medicare for All?

A proposal to replace Medicare premiums and cost sharing with unified national coverage

1. Part B Premium = Gone

Under proposed Medicare for All (MFA) legislation that eliminates premiums, the standard Medicare Part B premium would disappear. This is a description of a proposed system, not current law.

For comparison, the standard Part B premium in 2024 was $174.70 per month, with higher premiums for some beneficiaries under IRMAA, plus applicable deductibles and coinsurance.

Under the proposal described here:

  • All premiums are eliminated
  • All deductibles are eliminated
  • All copays are eliminated
  • All coinsurance is eliminated

    • Major Medicare for All proposals have generally aimed to eliminate point-of-service cost sharing for covered care, although details vary by bill and financing design.

    2. Why Part B Premiums Disappear

    The proposed national plan would replace or consolidate the roles of existing Medicare, Medicaid, employer insurance, marketplace plans, Medicare Advantage, Part D, supplemental plans, and much of the current out-of-network billing structure.

    Rather than separate Medicare Parts A, B, C, and D, the proposal envisions one national benefit covering medically necessary care. The existing Part B premium would no longer be charged.

    3. What People Pay Instead

    Healthcare would still require financing. Under the approach described in this article, premiums, deductibles, and copays would be replaced by a progressive tax structure, potentially including employer payroll contributions and household income-based taxes.

  • Employers would contribute through a predictable financing mechanism rather than buying private coverage.
  • Households would contribute according to the enacted tax rules rather than paying premiums and cost sharing for covered services.
  • The article proposes that 90% of households would pay less overall and employers would save 20–30% relative to current premiums; these are projected outcomes, not guarantees.

    • The actual effect on any household or business would depend on the legislation, tax rates, income, and existing insurance costs.

    4. Why Costs Could Drop Even Though Coverage Expands

    The proposal argues that national financing could reduce several costs associated with the present multipayer system:

  • Private insurer overhead
  • Duplicated billing administration
  • Prior-authorization bureaucracy
  • Multiple contract-negotiation teams
  • Claims-denial infrastructure
  • Marketing costs
  • Profit-taking associated with some insurance functions
  • Complexity across many payers

    • The supplied article cites estimated annual savings of $286.3 billion in administration, $377.5 billion from drug negotiation, and $295.6 billion from provider payment alignment, and a roughly $1.04 trillion overall annual savings estimate attributed to Yale School of Public Health modeling. These figures are scenario-dependent estimates and should not be treated as independently verified or additive without checking the original study assumptions.

    Bottom Line

  • Part B premium under the proposed Medicare for All system: $0.00
  • Premiums for covered care under this proposal: $0.00
  • Deductibles for covered care under this proposal: $0.00
  • Copays for covered care under this proposal: $0.00

    • The promise of the proposal is broader coverage without point-of-service charges. Whether total taxes plus remaining expenses would be lower for a particular person depends on how the plan is funded and implemented.

    Editorial note: The 2024 Part B premium is a historical figure, not the current premium. This article presents a proposed policy, not an enacted change to Medicare.
