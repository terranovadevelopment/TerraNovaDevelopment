When Help Exists on Paper but Not in Reality

Cathy’s Story and the Failure of America’s Housing System

Download Article #130 as Word document

There is something profoundly wrong with a society in which a 67-year-old disabled woman can spend six years living in her automobile while surrounded by one of the wealthiest civilizations in human history.

Her name is Cathy.

According to a story shared by Invisible People, Cathy gave up her home so she could bring her father home from a nursing facility. One month later, he died. What followed was six years of homelessness.

She now sleeps in her car with her dog, who provides companionship and a sense of security.

Eventually, Cathy received a housing voucher. On paper, help had finally arrived. But she could not find a landlord willing and able to rent her an affordable home before the voucher expired.

The system had provided assistance without providing a solution.

And that distinction tells us almost everything we need to know about what is wrong with our approach to homelessness.

A Housing Voucher Is Not a Home

We have constructed an extraordinary collection of programs, agencies, eligibility requirements, waiting lists, subsidies, vouchers, and regulations intended to help people experiencing homelessness.

But somewhere along the way, we have confused administering assistance with actually solving problems.

A housing voucher may represent a financial commitment. But if no suitable housing is available, if landlords refuse to participate, if rents exceed allowable limits, or if the recipient cannot navigate the process before a deadline, the voucher accomplishes nothing.

Cathy's experience illustrates this failure. Imagine telling someone who has been sleeping in a car for six years that they have finally qualified for housing assistance, only to discover that they must compete for apartments that may not exist within their budget.

Then imagine taking that assistance away because time has run out.

We should measure success by the number of people who are safely housed, not by the number of vouchers we distribute.

The purpose of a housing program is not to process paperwork. The purpose is to provide housing.

We Have Buildings. We Have Land. We Have the Technology.

Across America, we have underused office buildings, vacant commercial properties, aging motels, abandoned shopping centers, surplus public buildings, and land that could support new residential communities.

Not every building can be economically converted into housing. Some require demolition, substantial renovation, or entirely different uses. But many properties offer opportunities that deserve serious evaluation.

We have construction technology, modular housing systems, advanced manufacturing, engineering expertise, and enormous productive capacity.

We know how to build comfortable, accessible, energy-efficient homes. We know how to install plumbing, electricity, heating, cooling, kitchens, and bathrooms. We know how to make buildings safe for older adults and people with disabilities.

The question is not whether we know how to house people. The question is why we have failed to organize our resources around that objective.

Housing shortages are real. Construction costs are real. Land-use restrictions, financing challenges, and infrastructure limitations are real. But these are engineering, economic, and administrative problems. They are problems that human beings can solve. They are not laws of nature.

The Hidden Cost of Doing Nothing

When someone becomes homeless, the consequences extend far beyond the absence of a roof.

Living in a vehicle can mean inadequate sleep, exposure to extreme temperatures, difficulty maintaining hygiene, unreliable access to bathrooms, and constant anxiety about personal safety.

For older adults and people with disabilities, these conditions can be particularly dangerous.

Homelessness can aggravate existing medical problems, make treatment more difficult, and increase reliance on emergency services.

Meanwhile, communities spend money on temporary shelters, emergency medical care, outreach, policing, and administrative systems. These services can be necessary and lifesaving. But they are often being asked to manage a problem that permanent housing could help resolve.

A serious economic analysis should compare the full costs of homelessness with the costs of preventing it and providing stable housing.

In many circumstances, investing in housing and appropriate support services can reduce other public expenditures while dramatically improving people's lives.

We should stop treating homelessness as an unavoidable expense and begin treating its prevention as an investment in human well-being.

Give and Take Community Centers: A Practical Starting Point

This is one reason I have proposed a worldwide network of Give and Take Community Centers.

The idea is straightforward. Create welcoming community facilities operating 24 hours a day, seven days a week, where people can freely share resources and obtain assistance without navigating unnecessary obstacles.

A center could begin modestly, with tables and shelves in a safe, well-lit location where neighbors exchange food, clothing, hygiene products, household necessities, and other useful items.

As community partnerships and funding develop, the centers could expand into comprehensive support facilities.

Imagine what a properly equipped Give and Take Community Center could provide:

  • Nutritious meals, clean drinking water, showers, laundry facilities, and basic necessities.
  • Safe temporary accommodations and secure storage for personal belongings.
  • Assistance obtaining identification, benefits, medical care, and transportation.
  • Dedicated housing navigators who help people find actual apartments rather than simply handing them a list of telephone numbers.
  • Connections to mental health services, disability assistance, education, and employment opportunities.
  • Partnerships with property owners, builders, nonprofit organizations, and public agencies to create permanent housing.

    • And perhaps most importantly, imagine a system in which someone like Cathy would have a person responsible for helping her through the entire process.

    Not simply handing her a voucher. Not sending her to another office. Not telling her to call another telephone number. But remaining involved until she has a safe, accessible home.

    A community center cannot substitute for a permanent home. It can, however, become the place where immediate assistance and lasting solutions come together.

    Housing First. Dignity Always.

    We need to recognize a simple principle: A person should not have to become healthy, employed, sober, financially secure, or administratively sophisticated before becoming eligible for a safe place to live.

    Stable housing often makes it easier to address the other challenges in a person's life.

    Someone sleeping in a secure home has a better opportunity to manage medications, attend appointments, maintain personal hygiene, recover from illness, and participate in the community.

    Housing First approaches recognize that providing stable housing without unnecessary prerequisites can create the foundation for addressing other needs.

    But housing must be accompanied by appropriate, voluntary support services and a genuine supply of affordable homes.

    For Cathy, that means accessible housing that accommodates her disability and, wherever feasible, allows her to remain with the dog who helps her feel safe.

    We should design assistance around real human lives, not expect human lives to conform perfectly to administrative categories.

    A National Mission to End Homelessness

    Imagine if America approached homelessness with the same determination it once applied to electrification, interstate highways, and landing human beings on the Moon.

    We could begin with a national inventory of vacant and underused properties suitable for residential development.

    We could identify publicly owned land and buildings that could support affordable housing.

    We could accelerate responsible construction and adaptive reuse, expand modular housing production, and coordinate local governments with nonprofit and private developers.

    We could establish permanent housing pathways for older adults, veterans, people with disabilities, families with children, and everyone else experiencing homelessness.

    And we could create a coordinated network of Give and Take Community Centers to connect people with immediate assistance and long-term support.

    Such a program would require investment, competent management, transparent accountability, and cooperation across government, business, and civil society.

    But it would not require inventing some miraculous new technology. We already possess the knowledge and productive capabilities needed to make enormous progress. What we need is the determination to use them.

    What Would We Do If Cathy Were Our Mother?

    Sometimes the clearest way to understand a public problem is to make it personal.

    Imagine Cathy were your mother. She is 67 years old. She has a disability. She has spent six years sleeping in her automobile. She has a dog who keeps her company.

    She tried to obtain housing assistance. She received a voucher, but it expired before she could secure a home.

    Would you tell her the system had worked? Would you congratulate the agency for issuing the voucher? Would you tell her to be patient while another application is processed?

    Or would you ask the most obvious question in the world? Where is the home she needs, and what will it take to get her into it?

    That is the question our entire housing assistance system should be organized to answer. Not eventually. Not theoretically. But in reality.

    There Is No Them. There Is Only Us.

    Cathy reportedly offered a simple message: “Stop and talk to people. Some of us are really good people.”

    There is a heartbreaking truth in those words.

    People experiencing homelessness should not have to demonstrate exceptional goodness to deserve compassion.

    They should not have to explain their entire history to justify needing shelter. They should not have to prove that they are worthy of a bathroom, a meal, a bed, or a home.

    They are human beings. That is enough.

    We live in a civilization capable of producing extraordinary wealth, constructing magnificent buildings, developing artificial intelligence, exploring distant planets, and communicating instantly across the world.

    Surely we can organize ourselves to ensure that a 67-year-old woman does not have to spend six years sleeping in her car.

    This is not rocket science. We have the resources. We have the knowledge. We have the technology. We have people who genuinely care.

    What we need is the common sense and collective determination to put those resources together.

    Give and Take Community Centers could be one part of that solution. Affordable housing development, building reuse, prevention programs, and permanent supportive services must be other essential parts.

    The goal is not to create a more efficient system for managing homelessness. The goal is to end homelessness wherever we reasonably can, and to make it rare, brief, and nonrecurring where we cannot immediately prevent it.

    Cathy doesn't need another promise. She needs a home.

    And humanity needs to recognize that caring for people like Cathy is not charity bestowed upon strangers. It is simply what a civilized society does for its own people.

    There is no them. There is only us. And we take care of our own.

    Inspired by Cathy’s story as shared by Invisible People in Grants Pass, Oregon. Her experience is presented as reported in the supplied account.
