A Manifesto for Medicare for All

Why the Arguments Against It Are Misguided, Misleading, and Morally Incomplete

There comes a moment in every society when it must decide what kind of people it wishes to be. Not in slogans, not in speeches, but in the way it treats those who have the least power, the least wealth, and the least protection. In Matthew 25, Jesus does not ask us to judge our righteousness by how we treat the comfortable. He asks us to look at how we treat “the least of these.” And He tells us plainly: “Whatever you did for the least of my brethren, you did it for me.”

Medicare for All is not merely a policy proposal. It is a moral test. And the arguments raised against it — the fearmongering, the misdirection, the economic sleight-of-hand — collapse under the weight of that moral clarity.

I. The Fear of Job Loss: A Defense of Bureaucracy Disguised as Compassion

Opponents warn that Medicare for All will “destroy two million jobs.” But what jobs are they defending?

Jobs spent denying claims.
Jobs spent coding bills into arcane categories.
Jobs spent arguing with insurers.
Jobs spent chasing reimbursements.
Jobs spent selling plans designed to confuse rather than heal.

These are not jobs of mercy. They are jobs created by a broken system — jobs that count as GDP but produce nothing of real human value. They exist because suffering has been monetized. They exist because complexity has been weaponized. They exist because our system profits from fear.

To defend these jobs is to defend the machinery of delay, denial, and despair. It is to defend the very barriers that keep the poor from care. It is to defend the chains that bind “the least of these.”

II. The Cost Argument: A Mirage Built on Half-Truths

We are told Medicare for All is “too expensive.” Yet we already spend more than any nation on Earth — over $4.5 trillion a year — and still leave millions uninsured, underinsured, or drowning in medical debt.

We pay more and get less.
We pay more and fear more.
We pay more and heal less.

Medicare for All does not ask us to spend more. It asks us to spend better. It asks us to redirect money away from bureaucracy and toward care. It asks us to stop funding paperwork and start funding healing.

The cost argument is not an argument. It is a distraction — a way to make people forget that the current system is already unaffordable, already unsustainable, already unjust.

III. The Myth of “Choice”: A Marketing Slogan Masquerading as Freedom

Opponents claim Medicare for All “takes away choice.” But what choice do we have now?

The choice between plans designed to confuse.
The choice between networks designed to exclude.
The choice between deductibles designed to punish.
The choice between bankruptcy and treatment.

This is not freedom. It is a marketplace of fear.

Medicare for All does not take away choice. It takes away exploitation. It takes away fear. It takes away the illusion that choosing between bad options is the same as liberty.

IV. The Moral Argument: The One Opponents Cannot Answer

In Matthew 25, Jesus does not ask us to calculate premiums.
He does not ask us to defend insurance profits.
He does not ask us to preserve bureaucratic jobs.

He asks us one thing:
Did you care for the least of these?

Medicare for All is the only system that answers “yes” without hesitation.

It says:
No one will be turned away.
No one will be bankrupted.
No one will be left behind.
No one will suffer because they are poor.
No one will fear the bill more than the illness.

Opponents cannot answer this moral argument, so they hide behind economic myths, bureaucratic nostalgia, and fear-based talking points. But the truth remains: a system that bankrupts the sick is not moral. A system that denies care is not moral. A system that punishes poverty is not moral.

V. Liberation: The Promise Opponents Fear Most

Medicare for All is liberation.

Liberation from medical debt.
Liberation from fear.
Liberation from choosing between rent and treatment.
Liberation from jobs that exist only to deny care.
Liberation from a system that treats illness as a financial event.

Opponents fear this liberation because it threatens the profits built on suffering. It threatens the industries built on confusion. It threatens the belief that health care should be a commodity rather than a covenant.

VI. The Manifesto’s Conclusion: A Call to Moral Clarity

The arguments against Medicare for All are misguided at best and intentionally misleading at worst. They defend waste, not healing. They defend fear, not freedom. They defend bureaucracy, not compassion.

Medicare for All is not just an economic reform. It is a moral realignment. It is the moment when we choose to see every person — rich or poor, insured or uninsured, sick or well — as equally deserving of care, dignity, and protection.

It is the moment when we finally live the words of Matthew 25, not as metaphor, but as policy.

“Whatever you did for the least of these, you did it for me.”

This is the standard.
This is the calling.
This is the manifesto.
