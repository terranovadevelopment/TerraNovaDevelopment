Medicare for All and the Future of Home Care

Article #058 · November 11, 2026

Download Article #058 (Word document)

Introduction: A System Built for Institutions, Not Independence

America’s health-care system has long favored institutional care — hospitals, nursing homes, and assisted-living facilities — over the kind of home-based support that allows people to age and heal in dignity. For millions of seniors and veterans, this imbalance means losing independence, draining savings, and facing bureaucratic hurdles just to receive basic help at home.

Medicare for All (MFA) proposes a fundamental shift: to make home care a guaranteed right, not a privilege reserved for those who can afford private agencies or qualify for narrow programs.

1. The Current Landscape of Home Care

Today, home care in the United States is fragmented across multiple programs:

  • Medicare covers limited skilled home health services for eligible homebound patients; it generally does not cover ongoing custodial care.
  • Medicaid covers some long-term home- and community-based services, subject to eligibility and state program rules.
  • Private insurance rarely covers custodial or non-medical home care.
  • Veterans Affairs (VA) offers several home-based and long-term care benefits, subject to program eligibility; Aid & Attendance is one separate pension-related benefit.

This patchwork leaves millions without adequate support. Families often shoulder the burden, paying out-of-pocket or providing unpaid care that strains their finances and health.

2. What Medicare for All Changes

Under a comprehensive MFA proposal, home care could become a core benefit of the national health-care system. Every person — regardless of age, income, or disability status — would have access to:

  • Home health services (nursing, therapy, wound care, medication management)
  • Personal care assistance (help with bathing, dressing, eating, mobility)
  • Homemaker services (meal preparation, cleaning, errands)
  • Long-term services and supports (LTSS) for chronic conditions or disabilities
  • Respite care for family caregivers
  • Palliative and hospice care at home

The vision is to cover medically necessary and qualifying services without premiums, deductibles, or co-pays. Exact eligibility and covered services would depend on the legislation enacted and its implementing rules.

3. The Philosophy Behind MFA Home Care

MFA redefines health care as a continuum of support, not a series of isolated medical interventions. The guiding principles are:

  • Independence: People should be able to live safely and comfortably in their own homes.
  • Equity: Access to care should not depend on wealth or geography.
  • Integration: Medical and non-medical care should be coordinated seamlessly.
  • Dignity: Aging and disability are natural parts of life, not reasons for institutionalization.

Other countries with universal health systems, including Canada and Sweden, offer forms of publicly supported home care, although coverage and eligibility vary.

4. Economic and Social Impact

4.1. Cost Efficiency

For people who can be supported safely at home, home care can be less costly than institutional care. By shifting appropriate resources toward home-based services, MFA could:

  • Reduce avoidable hospital readmissions
  • Lower unnecessary emergency-room visits
  • Delay or prevent institutional placement
  • Reduce some long-term care costs

The scale of any savings would depend on staffing, care needs, program design, and implementation.

4.2. Workforce Development

MFA could strengthen the home-care workforce through:

  • Fair wages and benefits
  • Training and certification programs
  • Career pathways for caregivers and nurses

This strengthens both the economy and the quality of care.

4.3. Family and Community Stability

Families would face fewer impossible choices between work and caregiving. Communities could benefit from healthier, more independent elders and disabled residents who remain active and connected.

5. Veterans and Home Care Under MFA

Veterans stand to gain immensely from comprehensive home-based coverage. A well-designed MFA transition could:

  • Guarantee access to qualifying home-based care beyond existing VA pension pathways.
  • Coordinate VA services with the national system while protecting specialized veterans’ care.
  • Reduce confusion and predatory marketing around Aid & Attendance benefits.
  • Expand access to mental health, rehabilitation, and chronic-condition management at home.

Veterans’ choice of VA or civilian providers would depend on the final law and how VA services are preserved and integrated.

6. Challenges and Implementation

Transitioning to MFA home care requires:

  • Expanding the home-care workforce
  • Building national standards for quality and safety
  • Integrating electronic health records across providers
  • Ensuring rural access through mobile and telehealth services

These challenges are real, but solvable — and the payoff could be enormous: a system that values independence, compassion, and efficiency.

7. Conclusion: Home Is Where Health Belongs

Medicare for All could transform home care from a patchwork of programs into a pillar of universal health care. It recognizes that healing, aging, and living well often happen best in familiar surroundings — not institutions.

For seniors, veterans, and families alike, the goal is freedom from financial fear, bureaucratic barriers, and unnecessary displacement. It means a society that treats care as a right, not a commodity.
