America’s Hidden Healthcare Costs — The Deflationary Dynamo

How Medicare for All Could Create a Deflationary Dynamo Across the Economy

Policy perspective • Terra Nova Development

Most debates about Medicare for All get stuck on slogans and scare tactics. But the more useful question is where our healthcare dollars go today, how a universal system would be financed, and whether lower administrative and medical costs could benefit the wider economy. This is an argument about economic design as much as healthcare.

1. How We Currently Pay for Healthcare

Employers: Businesses pay substantial premiums to private insurers. Those costs affect compensation budgets, hiring, and competitiveness. The often-cited $15,000–$20,000 figure is not a universal per-employee cost; premiums vary by plan and whether coverage is individual or family.

Households: Families pay premiums, deductibles, copays, coinsurance, and sometimes unexpected bills. Even insured households can face medical debt.

Government: Federal and state programs already finance substantial care through Medicare, Medicaid, the VA, CHIP, ACA subsidies, and tax preferences for employer coverage.

We already spend enough as a nation to make universal coverage a serious policy option; the question is how to organize and finance it efficiently.

2. The Hidden Costs No One Talks About

Presenteeism and absenteeism: Illness can reduce productivity whether employees stay home or work while unwell.

Chronic disease mismanagement: Delayed or fragmented treatment can worsen diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and mental-health conditions.

Job lock: Employer-linked coverage can discourage workers from changing jobs or starting businesses.

Employer drag: Managing health benefits, eligibility, and insurer relationships consumes resources that could otherwise support core operations.

These costs are real economic considerations, although their precise size and how much Medicare for All would reduce them require careful modeling.

3. Why Costs Keep Rising

Multiple insurers maintain distinct coverage rules, billing requirements, and authorization processes. Fragmentation increases administrative burden.

Provider market concentration, drug pricing, negotiated rates, technology, labor costs, and incentives throughout the system all contribute to rising spending.

A single-payer approach could simplify payment and strengthen price negotiation, though it would not eliminate the underlying costs of delivering care.

4. Medicare for All Replaces How We Pay

Employers could stop purchasing conventional private health plans, but would likely face new taxes or contributions depending on the financing legislation.

Households could avoid premiums and point-of-service charges for covered services while paying through a revised tax structure. Net savings would vary by household.

Existing public programs could be coordinated or replaced under a unified benefit design, with transition rules for specialized systems and populations.

The crucial distinction is between total national healthcare spending and federal budget spending: public expenditure would increase even if overall spending fell.

5. The Potential Deflationary Effect on Everyday Prices

Home construction: Reduced employer health-benefit expenses could lower labor overhead and potentially affect home prices. Claims of $10,000–$25,000 savings per home should be treated as illustrative scenarios, not established forecasts.

Groceries: Grocery chains and suppliers might experience lower benefits-administration costs. Specific price reductions for milk, eggs, bread, meat, and produce cannot be predicted without knowing taxes, labor arrangements, competition, and pass-through.

Sporting events: Venues might save on some employer benefit costs, but ticket, food, parking, and merchandise prices also reflect market power, demand, rent, and other expenses.

The economic opportunity is not an automatic price cut on every product. It is a chance to reduce some embedded costs and allow competition to pass savings to consumers.

6. Medical and Dental Care: The Dynamo in Action

Simplified billing and fewer payer-specific rules could lower administrative spending for physicians, hospitals, and dental practices.

A universal benefits package could reduce coverage gaps and uncompensated care. Whether it eliminates prior authorization or denials depends on the exact rules adopted.

Illustrative comparisons sometimes show dental cleanings falling from $120 to $80–$90, crowns from $1,200–$1,600 to $900–$1,200, and office visits from $150–$250 to $90–$150. These figures are scenarios, not validated nationwide projections.

Provider payment rates, operating costs, service capacity, and the inclusion of dental benefits would determine actual outcomes.

7. The Six Gears of a Deflationary Dynamo

1. Clinics and hospitals spend less on redundant administration.

2. Employers spend less managing private coverage, net of replacement taxes.

3. Some business savings reach consumer prices through competition.

4. Some savings support wages and investment.

5. Households gain financial security from predictable healthcare costs.

6. Better access to timely care can improve health and productive capacity over time.

These are possible reinforcing effects, not a guarantee of permanent economy-wide deflation. The strongest defensible claim is that a well-designed system could lower healthcare cost growth and relieve pressure on other prices.

8. The Global Competitiveness Bonus

American manufacturers compete with firms in countries that finance healthcare differently. Other nations still impose healthcare costs through taxes, social insurance contributions, and wages; foreign employers do not universally pay zero.

Separating coverage from employment could make labor costs more predictable and reduce job lock. Some manufacturers could gain an advantage, depending on replacement taxes and industry conditions.

Stronger exports, reshoring, and smaller trade deficits are possible indirect benefits, not automatic results.

9. The Bottom Line

Medicare for All is an opportunity to redesign a fragmented system around universal access, simpler administration, and stronger bargaining power.

Potential benefits include less medical debt, improved access, reduced administrative waste, better workforce mobility, and lower total healthcare spending under some models.

Whether households, employers, providers, and the economy save money depends on benefit design, provider payment, taxation, implementation, and transition costs.

The central argument remains: healthcare reform should be judged by what Americans receive for what the nation spends—not by partisan slogans. A more efficient healthcare system could strengthen the entire economy.
