Medicare For All: A Practical Solution, Not a Political Battle

Download Word document

People often treat Medicare For All as a left-versus-right choice, but that framing misses the point entirely. I see it as a practical solution to a national problem, not an ideological fight. Politics should be set aside because Medicare For All benefits everyone — rich or poor, employer or employee, insured or uninsured. It is not a partisan idea or a socialist idea; it is simply a smarter, more efficient way to deliver healthcare while strengthening the entire economy.

The United States spends nearly twice as much on healthcare as other developed nations, yet delivers worse access, worse outcomes, and more financial stress. When you combine the direct costs with the indirect, hidden costs, the current system becomes one of the most expensive and inefficient structures in the modern world.

Medicare For All is not radical. The current system is.

1. The Direct Costs: Already Higher Than Medicare For All

America spends almost $5 trillion per year on healthcare. That includes premiums, deductibles, employer contributions, Medicare and Medicaid, hospital charges, and private insurance overhead.

As one of your documents puts it: “The current system wastes staggering sums on activities that produce no health care whatsoever.”

A single-payer system eliminates private insurance overhead, aligns payment rates, and negotiates drug prices nationally. Multiple economic models show that Medicare For All would reduce total U.S. health spending by over $1 trillion annually, even while covering everyone.

That is not ideology. That is math.

2. The Indirect Costs: The Hidden Tax No One Sees

The direct costs are only half the story. The current system quietly imposes massive indirect costs on employers and workers — costs that never appear on a medical bill but hit every balance sheet in the country.

Presenteeism — Workers show up sick because they can’t afford care. Cost: $4,000–$6,000 per worker per year.

Absenteeism — Workers miss work due to untreated conditions. Cost: $225.8 billion annually.

Stress-Related Illness — Financial and insurance stress convert directly into physical illness. Cost: approximately $300 billion annually.

Chronic Disease Mismanagement — Conditions worsen when people avoid care due to cost. Cost: $100+ billion annually.

Employer Administrative Burden — HR departments, billing teams, and compliance staff are required because the system is fragmented. Cost: tens of billions annually.

Job Lock — Workers stay in jobs they don’t want because they fear losing coverage. Cost: lost innovation, entrepreneurship, and mobility.

Wage Suppression — Premiums rise faster than wages, eating compensation budgets. Cost: every paycheck in America.

Economy-Wide Price Inflation — Healthcare inflation becomes general inflation because healthcare costs are baked into everything.

“The hidden costs of the current system are not a rounding error. They are a structural tax on workforce performance.” Medicare For All removes that tax.

3. The Biggest Downward Pressure: Employers and Employees Stop Paying Premiums

The largest deflationary force in Medicare For All comes from one structural change: employers and employees stop paying premiums and out-of-pocket costs.

Right now: employers pay $6,300–$7,600 per worker for single coverage; employers pay approximately $20,000 per worker for family coverage; workers pay $1,400–$6,800 in premiums; workers pay $3,000–$12,000 in deductibles; and workers face $5,000–$12,000 in out-of-pocket exposure.

This is $1.8–$2.0 trillion in combined employer and employee spending every year.

Under Medicare For All? All of that disappears. Not reduced. Not subsidized. Not restructured. Gone.

This is the true deflationary engine: businesses face lower operating costs; prices fall because the cost of labor falls; wages rise because compensation budgets are freed; workers gain disposable income; consumer demand increases; debt burdens fall; and financial stress collapses.

This is downward pressure on prices across the entire economy, not just healthcare. No version of the current system can produce this effect. Only Medicare For All can.

4. The Global Competitiveness Gap

American businesses pay $15,000–$20,000 per employee per year for healthcare — a cost their competitors in Canada, Germany, Japan, and the UK do not bear.

This is a self-inflicted competitive disadvantage. Medicare For All removes it instantly.

5. The Ideology Trap: A Manufactured Distraction

Opponents want Americans to believe Medicare For All is a socialist project. It isn’t. As your document states: “The math does not have a party.”

Medicare For All is simply the cheaper, more efficient, more competitive system. Every other developed nation has already figured this out.

Conclusion: A No-Brainer for America

Medicare For All is not socialism. It is not radical. It is not partisan.

It is cheaper, more efficient, better for business, better for workers, better for innovation, better for global competitiveness, and better for the entire economy.

The current system costs nearly twice as much once you include indirect costs. It suppresses wages, inflates prices, and drags down productivity. Medicare For All reverses all of that.

Whether you call yourself a Democratic Socialist or not, the conclusion is the same: Medicare For All makes total sense. It is a no-brainer. It is good for the USA.
