The Master Plan of Our Creator

Chaos by Design, Order by Effort, and Humanity’s Journey Toward Divine Unity

Download Article #135 as Word document

There is a possibility so magnificent that, once we begin to contemplate it, the entire story of human existence takes on a different meaning.

What if the world was never intended to be a finished paradise? What if the chaos, uncertainty, struggle, and painful history of humanity are part of a greater process through which we learn, grow, create, reconcile, and ultimately bring the world into harmony with the divine will of our Creator?

What if the purpose of human existence is not simply to survive this world, escape it, or endure it until something better comes along? What if we are here to make this world everything it can be?

This is the vision at the heart of what I believe may be the Master Plan of our Creator: not perpetual suffering or eternal division, but creation unfolding through the conscious participation of the beings God created, until humanity and its Creator are united through understanding and love.

1. A World Created with Possibility Rather Than Completion

Consider the world into which humanity was born. Mountains rise and erode. Rivers flood and change course. Forests grow, burn, and regenerate. Stars form and galaxies evolve over billions of years. Creation is not motionless. It is alive with change.

Human civilization has followed the same pattern. Through observation, experimentation, cooperation, and countless generations of effort, we learned to cultivate food, construct shelter, heal disease, harness energy, and communicate across continents.

Every achievement required humanity to confront something it did not yet understand. Every discovery transformed uncertainty into knowledge. Every invention brought a measure of order to the world.

Perhaps the unfinished nature of creation is precisely what gives human existence its purpose. God did not give us a completed world merely to admire. He gave us a world of possibilities and the capacity to participate in their fulfillment.

2. Chaos Is the Beginning, Not the Destination

We generally regard chaos as something negative. We associate it with confusion, conflict, destruction, uncertainty, and fear. But chaos can also describe possibilities not yet organized into meaningful structure: a field before it becomes a garden, materials before they become a home, a question before it becomes a discovery.

Human history has been filled with terrible disorder: wars, exploitation, poverty, persecution, disease, and needless suffering. We should never romanticize those tragedies or suggest that cruelty was necessary or divinely commanded.

Yet even from humanity’s darkest experiences, we can learn why institutions failed, why people turned against one another, and how future generations might avoid repeating those mistakes. Chaos is not something we are commanded to preserve. It is something we are called to overcome.

Chaos may be our starting condition. Harmony is the objective.

3. The Divine Will Is Not Separate from Human Responsibility

Throughout history, people have prayed for peace, healing, justice, and an end to hunger and suffering. But what if the answer to many of those prayers is already present in the abilities God has given us?

We possess intelligence, imagination, compassion, and the ability to cooperate, invent, organize, and transform our surroundings. Today we also possess technologies that would have seemed miraculous to earlier generations.

If people are hungry, we should feed them. If people are homeless, we should house them. If people are sick, we should heal them. If people are ignorant, we should educate them. If people are divided, we should bring them together. If our institutions create unnecessary suffering, we should redesign them.

These are not merely political or economic objectives. They can be understood as spiritual responsibilities. We align ourselves with our Creator not simply by proclaiming our faith, but by participating in the improvement of His creation.

4. Humanity’s History Is a Lesson, Not a Life Sentence

We have fought wars over territory, religion, ethnicity, ideology, and wealth. We have enslaved and exploited one another, divided ourselves into categories of superiority and inferiority, and tolerated preventable suffering because institutions resisted change.

But history is a record of what humanity has done, not a decree about what humanity must always do. The mistakes of previous generations can become knowledge that enables future generations to choose differently.

Reconciliation does not require pretending injustice never occurred. It begins with acknowledging truth, repairing harm wherever possible, establishing justice, and refusing to perpetuate the conditions that produced injury.

We cannot undo every tragedy. We can refuse to make those tragedies the permanent foundation of civilization. The past must be understood, its victims honored, its injustices addressed, and its lessons carried forward—but its divisions need not be inherited forever.

5. The Great Reconciliation

Imagine a future in which humanity finally understands that the divisions separating us were never as fundamental as we believed. Nations would retain their cultures, people their individuality, and communities their histories. But difference would no longer require hostility, individuality would no longer require isolation, and diversity would no longer be mistaken for separation.

The suffering of another person would no longer be dismissed as someone else’s problem. The success of another community would no longer be regarded as a threat. The prosperity of one nation would no longer require the deprivation of another.

We would organize civilization around a simple realization: There is no them. There is only us. Reconciliation is not the elimination of difference, but the recognition that our differences exist within a greater unity.

6. Technology as an Instrument of Divine Purpose

Artificial intelligence can help us analyze problems of enormous complexity. Robotics can perform dangerous and repetitive work. Modern agriculture can produce extraordinary quantities of food. Renewable energy can help power communities. Global communications can connect people across borders. Medical science continues to expand our ability to relieve suffering.

These technologies do not automatically create justice or abundance. They require resources, wise decisions, and responsible governance. They can be misused as easily as they can be used for good. But their potential is extraordinary.

Imagine directing humanity’s productive and intellectual capacity toward ensuring that every person has access to the essentials of a dignified life: education available everywhere, healthcare organized around healing, useful goods shared instead of discarded, and housing designed around human need.

Technology becomes an instrument of divine purpose when directed by wisdom, compassion, and the determination to improve life for everyone.

7. The Kingdom of God as a Living Reality

Jesus taught his followers to pray: ‘Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven.’ — Matthew 6:10, King James Version.

That prayer does not merely ask humanity to abandon the Earth. It asks that God’s will be accomplished here. What would a world aligned with the divine will look like? Would children go hungry? Would elderly people sleep in automobiles because they could not afford homes? Would the sick be denied necessary treatment? Would nations destroy one another over resources they could share?

I cannot believe such conditions represent the ultimate fulfillment of God’s intentions for creation. I envision a world characterized by compassion, cooperation, justice, and peace, in which human beings recognize their responsibility to one another.

Perhaps the Kingdom of God is not only something to await. Perhaps we are also called to prepare the world to reflect it.

8. The Apocalypse as the Great Revealing

The Greek word apokalypsis, from which we derive apocalypse, means an unveiling or revelation. What if the great revelation is not simply the destruction of the world we know, but the disclosure of truths we have failed to recognize?

What if humanity finally sees the underlying unity of creation? What if we discover that much of our conflict arose from misunderstanding, fear, and the illusion of separation? What if the final reconciliation is recognition that we have always existed within the presence of our Creator?

The Book of Revelation presents a vision of a new heaven and a new earth, with God dwelling among humanity. Revelation 21:4 speaks of the removal of death, mourning, crying, and pain. Christians interpret these passages in different ways, and no one can establish a timetable for their fulfillment.

But the hope they express is unmistakable: suffering and estrangement do not have the final word. The ultimate destination is reconciliation, renewal, and communion between creation and its Creator.

9. Paradise Is Not an Excuse to Abandon the World

Some spiritual thinking regards the material world as something inferior to endure until we can escape. Consider another possibility: this world is where we develop the understanding, compassion, and wisdom that prepare us for deeper unity with God.

When we learn to love, we become more capable of love. When we practice forgiveness, we become more capable of reconciliation. When we build systems that serve others, we develop the habits of cooperation. When we overcome fear and division, we become more capable of recognizing unity.

Paradise, in this interpretation, is not something we purchase through good works or earn through suffering. It is the fulfillment of a relationship with our Creator—a relationship whose character we begin to understand through love, service, and the responsible exercise of freedom.

We do not improve creation to make ourselves worthy of God’s love. We improve creation because love calls us to do so.

10. The Master Plan: From Chaos to Conscious Unity

Creation begins with extraordinary potential. Human beings emerge with consciousness, curiosity, imagination, and freedom. We encounter beauty and difficulty. We learn through experience, discover natural laws, develop technologies, build civilizations, make mistakes, and experience their consequences.

We gradually recognize the suffering of others and discover that cooperation can accomplish what conflict cannot. Through this process, we become increasingly capable of understanding the unity underlying existence.

Perhaps this is the Master Plan—not a mechanical sequence in which every tragedy was predetermined, nor a justification for suffering humanity inflicted on itself, but a divine invitation: creation filled with possibility, inhabited by beings capable of freedom, and directed toward the fulfillment of love.

The objective is not merely order in our physical environment. It is harmony in our relationships, justice in our institutions, wisdom in our decisions, compassion in our actions, and unity in our understanding of existence.

11. The Future We Are Called to Create

Imagine the day when humanity looks back upon our present condition with astonishment. Future generations may wonder why we allowed hunger while food could be produced and distributed, homelessness while buildings stood unused, and healthcare to depend so heavily on financial circumstances.

They may wonder why education was limited by geography or wealth, why nations invested so much ingenuity in preparing to destroy one another, and why fear separated people who shared the same planet and fundamental hopes.

Perhaps they will understand that we were living through a transitional period, when our technologies had advanced faster than our institutions and the old divisions were becoming difficult to justify.

Perhaps the transformation began when ordinary people asked a different question: What would the world look like if we finally aligned our actions with the highest purposes of creation?

Conclusion: The Rainbow Beyond the Storm

Look at the image accompanying this article. A city stands beneath a turbulent sky. The streets are crowded. The world appears chaotic and uncertain. Yet above the city stretches a rainbow. The storm has not necessarily ended, but something beautiful has appeared above the disorder: a promise and a possibility.

We were not placed here merely to observe the world. We were given the capacity to transform it. We were not given intelligence merely to compete, but to understand and cooperate. We were not given freedom merely to pursue ourselves, but to choose love.

The chaos of history can give way to the order of understanding. The wounds of the past can become the beginning of reconciliation. The divisions of humanity can yield to recognition of shared existence.

Chaos by design. Order by effort. Unity through love.

Perhaps the Master Plan has been unfolding all along. Perhaps our responsibility is to recognize it, align ourselves with it, and participate wholeheartedly in its fulfillment. The future is not something we must simply await. It is something we are called to help create.

And at the end of that journey, perhaps we will discover that humanity’s greatest achievement was not the conquest of nature, accumulation of wealth, or mastery of technology, but the moment we finally understood who we are: children of one Creator, participants in one creation, members of one human family.

There is no them. There is only us. And together, in harmony with the divine will of our Creator, we can make this world everything it was meant to be.
