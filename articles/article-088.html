Walmart: What Employer-Provided Healthcare Really Costs

Download Word document

A case study in the business argument for Medicare for All

America’s Largest Private Employer

Walmart is one of the largest private employers in the United States, with approximately 1.6 million U.S. employees in the figures used for this example. Its scale illustrates the enormous cost of employer-sponsored healthcare and the financial stakes for both companies and workers.

What Walmart Spends on Healthcare

The supplied estimates place Walmart’s annual employee healthcare spending at more than $6 billion. The figure is described as including employer contributions, premiums, administrative expenses, and wellness programs. Healthcare is presented as one of the company’s largest employee-related expenses after wages and payroll taxes.

Even a company with Walmart’s purchasing power cannot escape the complexity and expense of a fragmented insurance system. Money spent administering and financing coverage is money that cannot simultaneously be used for wages, hiring, capital investment, or lower prices.

What Walmart Employees Spend

The proposed case study uses the following illustrative employee cost ranges:

  • Annual employee premium contribution: $1,200–$1,800.
  • Average deductible: $2,700–$3,500.
  • Annual out-of-pocket maximum: $5,000–$7,000.
  • Estimated collective employee spending: $2–$3 billion per year on premiums and out-of-pocket care.

These categories must be interpreted carefully. A deductible or out-of-pocket maximum is a potential liability, not necessarily an amount each worker actually pays. Employee spending also depends on enrollment, plan selection, family coverage, and healthcare use.

The Combined Burden

Using the estimates above, Walmart and its employees are portrayed as spending approximately $8–$9 billion annually on healthcare. This is an illustrative combined figure, not a verified accounting total. Employer contributions and employee expenses should be measured for the same period and without double counting.

Why This Example Matters Beyond Walmart

Other major employers — including Amazon, UPS, Home Depot, FedEx, Target, General Motors, Ford, Boeing, Delta, and Marriott — also face substantial costs associated with employee health benefits. Their workforce sizes, benefit structures, and expenses differ, but the underlying challenge is common: healthcare financing is a major business cost, and workers often bear premiums and out-of-pocket exposure as well.

What Medicare for All Could Change

Under a Medicare for All proposal, employer-sponsored premiums and many patient charges would be replaced by publicly financed coverage. The healthcare itself would still need to be paid for, and businesses and households could face different taxes or contributions. Whether a particular company or worker pays less would depend on the legislation and its financing design.

A well-designed single-payer system could reduce billing complexity, improve access to care, and give employers more predictable healthcare-related costs. It could also make it easier for workers to change jobs or start businesses without losing coverage. These potential gains should be weighed against transition expenses, provider-payment decisions, and the costs of expanded services.

Conclusion: A Business Question, Not Just a Political One

The Walmart example helps make an abstract national debate concrete. The employer-based system imposes significant costs on companies and employees alike. Medicare for All deserves rigorous consideration not only as a question of healthcare access but as a question of business efficiency, economic competitiveness, and financial security.

The central question is not whether healthcare has a cost. It is whether America can finance better healthcare for everyone at a lower overall cost and with fewer burdens on workers and employers.

Editorial note: Workforce size, company spending, employee contributions, deductibles, and the $8–$9 billion combined estimate are drawn from the author’s supplied draft and have not been independently verified against Walmart filings or plan documents. They should be confirmed before being presented as established facts. Broad claims about CEO or bipartisan support also require supporting evidence.
