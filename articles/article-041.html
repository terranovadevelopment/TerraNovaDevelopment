The Digital Commons: National Internet and Cellular Infrastructure

Why America Should Treat Internet and Cellular Connectivity as National Infrastructure

A Terra Nova Development policy vision | November 2026

Introduction: Connectivity Is Essential Infrastructure

Internet and cellular service are no longer luxuries. They are the pathways through which Americans learn, work, receive healthcare, operate businesses, communicate in emergencies, and participate in civic life. Yet access remains uneven, prices can be burdensome, and providers often build overlapping infrastructure while rural communities remain underserved. A national digital infrastructure strategy could treat connectivity much as the country treats highways: a shared foundation supporting competition, commerce, and opportunity.

1. The Economic Case for Universal Connectivity

Broadband expansion can support productivity, remote employment, education, telehealth, new businesses, and higher property values. The proposal motivating this article cites estimates of $59.7 billion in investment associated with $146 billion in GDP activity, increases of 14–17% in home values with fiber access, $1,450 in additional annual income for some nonurban households, and $3.24 trillion in potential nationwide fiber benefits. These figures are illustrative claims from the proposal, not independently verified forecasts; their methods and applicability should be reviewed before use in legislation.

The central economic logic is sound as a policy hypothesis: when reliable broadband reaches more households and enterprises, it can reduce barriers to participation in the modern economy. Universal connectivity would give a small business in a remote town access to many of the same digital markets as a company in a major city.

2. National Security and Resilience

Communications networks underpin emergency response, hospitals, utilities, logistics, financial services, and government operations. A coordinated national strategy could invest in redundant routes, stronger cybersecurity standards, resilient power, interoperable systems, and rapid restoration after disasters. Public stewardship of critical backbone assets may improve coordination, but concentration of infrastructure also creates single points of failure. Any national network must be decentralized in its engineering, independently audited, and designed for continuity under attack or natural disaster.

3. A Public Backbone with Competitive Private Services

The proposed model separates infrastructure from retail service. A public or publicly governed wholesale backbone would offer fair, nondiscriminatory access to private internet and mobile providers. Those companies would compete on customer support, reliability, service features, speed, and innovation rather than exclusively on ownership of essential routes.

This resembles the public-highway/private-trucking relationship, though telecommunications are technically and legally more complex. Existing fiber, towers, radio access networks, last-mile connections, and licensed spectrum do not all lend themselves to the same ownership model. A workable plan should distinguish national transport infrastructure from local distribution and wireless spectrum.

4. A Fair Transition for Existing Companies

A transition should protect service continuity, customers, employees, investors, and taxpayers. Options include negotiated purchases of selected infrastructure at independently assessed fair market value; voluntary asset transfers; long-term operating concessions; public-private partnerships; and open-access obligations on publicly financed networks.

Rather than assuming that all carriers would benefit automatically, policymakers should model which assets remain privately owned, how legacy debts are handled, how wholesale rates are set, and whether providers have incentives to invest. Spectrum licenses are subject to distinct federal rules and cannot simply be treated as ordinary physical property. Antitrust, competition, and constitutional considerations require careful review.

5. Financing the Digital Commons

Possible funding sources include federal infrastructure appropriations, municipal participation, long-term wholesale access fees, private co-investment, and targeted bonds. Public funds should prioritize underserved areas, resilience, and open access. Revenue assumptions must be stress-tested against maintenance, upgrades, cybersecurity, energy, and eventual replacement costs.

The proposal also references up to $20 trillion in private investment potentially unlocked by a national plan. That is an extraordinary projection and should not be treated as an established financing source without an identifiable study, time horizon, and economic model.

6. How to Implement the Plan

Begin with a national infrastructure inventory and an independent feasibility assessment. Identify where competition is effective, where networks are duplicated, and where communities remain unserved. Launch regional open-access pilots with clear performance and affordability targets. Develop transparent wholesale pricing, enforce strong privacy protections, and maintain independent oversight. Expand only after pilots demonstrate measurable public benefits.

Success should be judged by household affordability, uptime, speed, latency, rural coverage, business formation, emergency resilience, customer satisfaction, and total lifecycle cost—not merely miles of fiber installed.

7. The Promise—and the Tradeoffs

A shared national backbone could reduce some duplication, expand access, and give private companies new ways to compete. It could also introduce risks: political interference, procurement waste, weak maintenance incentives, market concentration, and costly acquisitions. Public ownership is a governance choice, not an automatic guarantee of lower prices. The strongest model would combine universal-service obligations, transparent accounting, independent regulation, open technical standards, and meaningful competition wherever possible.

Conclusion: A Connected Nation

The long-term vision is a nation in which every person can reach education, employment, healthcare, public services, and one another without connectivity becoming a barrier. The United States built roads, electric grids, and water systems because access to foundational infrastructure expands freedom and economic opportunity. It is time to consider digital connectivity in the same category.

A national digital commons need not eliminate private enterprise. Designed carefully, it could give innovators a stronger platform on which to build while ensuring that no community is left disconnected from the future.

Policy note

This article is a forward-looking policy proposal. Numerical claims supplied in the originating concept have not been independently validated and should be sourced before publication as established facts. Economic gains, savings, and carrier profitability depend on implementation and are not guaranteed.
