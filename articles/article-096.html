The Great Contrast: Medicare for All Compared to Our Current System

Why Our Current Health-Care System Is a Labyrinth — and Medicare for All Is a Straight Line

The United States does not have a health-care “system.” It has a patchwork, a maze, a tangle of overlapping programs, private intermediaries, billing codes, networks, and profit-driven rules that no single human being fully understands. It is a structure built by accident, lobbying, and historical drift — not by design.

Medicare for All, by contrast, is a system: one program, one payer, one set of rules, one card, one standard of care. The difference between the two is not incremental. It is civilizational.

Below is a detailed comparison of the complexity of our current system versus the simplicity of Medicare for All.

I. The Current System: A Fragmented Maze

1. Multiple Programs, Each With Different Rules

Today, Americans must navigate:

Employer insurance

Individual marketplace plans

Medicaid

Medicare Part A

Medicare Part B

Medicare Part C (Advantage)

Medicare Part D

CHIP

VA

TRICARE

Workers’ compensation

COBRA

Short-term plans

Supplemental Medigap plans

Each has:

different eligibility rules

different enrollment windows

different networks

different deductibles

different copays

different coverage exclusions

different appeal processes

This is not a system. It is a bureaucratic jungle.

2. Private Insurance Adds Another Layer of Chaos

Every private insurer has:

its own billing codes

its own reimbursement rates

its own prior-authorization rules

its own denial triggers

its own documentation requirements

its own network restrictions

its own appeal process

A doctor’s office may deal with 20–40 insurers every week, each with different rules.

This forces hospitals to hire massive billing departments — sometimes larger than their nursing staff.

3. Coding Has Become a Monster

The U.S. uses:

70,000+ ICD-10 diagnosis codes

10,000+ CPT procedure codes

insurer-specific modifiers

insurer-specific documentation rules

insurer-specific “medical necessity” definitions

Coding is no longer a record of care.

It is a battlefield.

4. Prior Authorization Is a System of Delays

Providers must constantly ask permission to treat patients:

MRIs

surgeries

medications

specialist referrals

Insurers deny or delay millions of requests every year.

Doctors spend hours fighting these denials.

Patients suffer while waiting.

5. Networks Restrict Care

Patients must constantly ask:

“Is this doctor in-network?”

“Is this hospital covered?”

“Will my plan pay for this?”

Out-of-network care can bankrupt a family.

6. Billing Is a Nightmare

Hospitals spend 25% of their budget on billing and insurance paperwork.

Patients receive:

surprise bills

facility fees

out-of-network charges

denied claims

balance bills

incomprehensible statements

Billing is not a side task.

It is an industry.

7. The Human Cost

The complexity of the system creates:

medical debt

delayed care

fear of bankruptcy

skipped medications

untreated conditions

stress and anxiety

lost productivity

worse health outcomes

This complexity is not accidental.

It is profitable.

II. Medicare for All: A Unified, Simple System

Medicare for All replaces the maze with a single, universal program.

1. One Payer

Under MFA:

one card

one program

one set of rules

one billing portal

one reimbursement schedule

one documentation standard

Every provider interacts with the same system.

2. No Networks

You can see any licensed provider.

No in-network.

No out-of-network.

No surprise bills.

3. No Prior Authorization Games

Medically necessary care is covered.

Doctors treat patients — not insurers.

4. Coding Becomes Simple

Coding still exists, but:

one set of codes

one payer

no insurer-specific modifiers

no denial games

no multi-layer documentation battles

Coding becomes record-keeping, not warfare.

5. Billing Departments Shrink

Administrative overhead drops from:

15–20% (current system)

to

2–3% (single-payer norms)

Hospitals redirect resources to:

nurses

aides

mental-health staff

primary care

community health

6. Universal Coverage

Everyone is covered:

no premiums

no deductibles

no copays for essential care

no medical debt

no bankruptcy from illness

Coverage becomes a human right, not a financial gamble.

7. Providers Get Predictable Payments

Under MFA:

every claim is paid

every claim is paid on time

every claim is paid at the same rate

no insurer games

no reimbursement chaos

Predictability replaces chaos.

8. The Moral Dimension

Medicare for All aligns with the moral duty of Matthew 25:

“Whatever you did for the least of these, you did it for me.”

A system that bankrupts the sick is not moral.

A system that denies care is not moral.

A system that punishes poverty is not moral.

Medicare for All is not just simpler.

It is more just.

III. The Core Difference: Complexity vs. Humanity

The Current System

fragmented

confusing

profit-driven

administratively bloated

morally incoherent

financially punishing

structurally chaotic

Medicare for All

unified

simple

predictable

efficient

morally aligned

financially stable

structurally elegant

The current system is a labyrinth designed by insurers.

Medicare for All is a straight line designed for people.

Conclusion: The Complexity We Have vs. the Simplicity We Deserve

The U.S. health-care system is not complex because it must be.

It is complex because complexity is profitable.

Medicare for All is not simple because it is naive.

It is simple because simplicity is humane.

One system heals.

The other confuses.

One system liberates.

The other entangles.

One system honors “the least of these.”

The other burdens them.

The contrast is not technical.

It is moral.
