The $70 MRI Question: What Medicare for All Could Change

The $70 MRI Question

What an international price comparison reveals about U.S. healthcare costs—and what Medicare for All could change

Concept image supplied for this article; it is AI-generated and does not document an actual patient, scan, or price.

1. What the China MRI example reveals

Consider the striking claim that an MRI in China can cost around $70 while a U.S. patient may be quoted $1,200 to $3,000. Those figures are illustrative, not a verified apples-to-apples comparison: scan type, facility, geography, contrast use, insurance contracts and billing arrangements can change the price dramatically. Yet the comparison raises a legitimate question: why are American medical prices so variable and often so high?

The U.S. system involves negotiations among hospitals, insurers, physician groups and other intermediaries. Coding, prior authorizations, claims disputes and collection systems all consume resources. Hospitals also face substantial equipment, staffing, compliance and uncompensated-care costs. The result is a complex price structure that patients often cannot predict.

2. How Medicare for All could change the equation

Unified payment and negotiated prices

A national single-payer program could establish standardized reimbursement schedules and negotiate prices at scale. The exact MRI payment would depend on legislation and implementation; Medicare reimbursement is not one universal fixed price.

Simpler administration

Replacing multiple billing rules with a unified payer could reduce some insurance-related paperwork, billing disputes and administrative overhead. It would not eliminate the need for medical records, clinical documentation, fraud controls or operational administration.

Access without point-of-service charges

A Medicare for All proposal with no patient premiums, deductibles or copayments could reduce financial barriers to necessary imaging. It would not automatically guarantee immediate appointment availability or eliminate clinical authorization and capacity constraints.

3. The hidden costs of delayed care

When people postpone a medically indicated MRI because of price, diagnosis and treatment may be delayed. Some delays can lead to worse outcomes and higher subsequent costs, although not every scan is necessary and earlier testing does not always save money. A well-designed universal system would need evidence-based imaging guidelines alongside affordable access.

Employers also face costs from employee illness, absenteeism, reduced productivity and benefits administration. Those costs are real, but figures such as $5,500–$7,200 per employee vary by study, definition and population and should not be treated as guaranteed savings from a particular reform.

4. Would Medicare for All be deflationary?

Standardized rates, simplified billing and stronger purchasing leverage could lower certain prices and slow healthcare spending growth. Total national savings would depend on provider payments, utilization, drug prices, taxes, transition costs and the benefits covered. More access may also increase use of needed services. Claims of $1 trillion in annual savings are estimates from particular models, not settled outcomes.

5. Comparing the three approaches

Current U.S. system

Multiple payers, highly variable billed and negotiated prices, employer-linked insurance for many people, and significant billing complexity.

Reported China example

An approximately $70 MRI is a striking anecdote, but it requires verification of the procedure, date, location and full charges before drawing direct conclusions.

Proposed Medicare for All

Universal coverage and nationally determined payment rules could improve price predictability and reduce financial barriers, with financing and access effects depending on the final design.

Conclusion: A question of system design

If a patient can obtain comparable, high-quality diagnostic imaging abroad at a fraction of the U.S. price, policymakers should examine why. The useful lesson is not that any one country has solved every healthcare problem. It is that prices, administration and access are shaped by choices about how healthcare is financed and delivered. Medicare for All is one proposed structural response, and its benefits and tradeoffs deserve careful, evidence-based examination.
