The Homes We Already Have

Why America’s Affordable Housing Future Begins With Buildings, Land, and Common Sense

Download Article #127 as Word document

We have an affordable housing crisis, but we also have something remarkable: an enormous inventory of buildings, parking lots, office complexes, vacant retail properties, government facilities, and underused land. These are not automatically ready-made homes. Yet they represent an opportunity hiding in plain sight. Before we conclude that every family must wait for an entirely new neighborhood to be built, why not look carefully at the structures and locations we already possess?

A building that has outlived its original purpose has not necessarily outlived its usefulness. It may be an obsolete office today and the foundation of a thriving residential community tomorrow. This is not a fantasy about the distant future. It is a practical design question: what is the highest and best human use of the assets we already have?

1. New York City Offers a Provocative Example

On September 22, 2026, New York City announced the start of public review for a proposal to redevelop 100 Gold Street in Lower Manhattan. The city-owned site, now occupied by an aging government office building, is proposed to become a mixed-income residential development with approximately 4,000 homes, including about 1,000 permanently affordable, rent-stabilized apartments. The project also envisions an improved older-adult center, public-space improvements, and a publicly accessible fitness facility. GFP Real Estate was selected to lead the development. [1][2]

The financing proposal is especially interesting: according to the city, the project would use revenues from the larger mixed-income development to help support the affordable apartments, rather than require direct City funding for the development. City land, development rights, applicable tax policy, and market conditions still matter; “no City funding” does not mean the project requires no valuable public contribution. Nor is this a completed housing project. Environmental and land-use review remain ahead, including anticipated land-use review in 2027. [1][2][3]

There is another important distinction: the 100 Gold Street plan calls for replacing the existing office building with new construction. It is not simply a plan to cut an old skyscraper into apartments. That distinction does not weaken the idea. It proves that adaptive reuse is bigger than one construction method: an underused site can be converted, renovated, expanded, or rebuilt to produce far more public benefit.

2. The Question Every City Should Be Asking

How many properties in your community are substantially underused while residents struggle to find an affordable home? Walk through a downtown on a weekday evening. Look at aging office towers with persistently vacant floors, empty big-box stores, deteriorating motels, unused school buildings, former hospitals, obsolete industrial parcels, and vast surface parking lots. Some sites are unsuitable for housing; others could be excellent candidates. The key is to stop treating every empty or obsolete property as someone else’s problem.

Cities should create a public inventory of potentially reusable buildings and parcels, with clear information about ownership, zoning, structural condition, transit access, utilities, environmental risks, and rough conversion or redevelopment costs. An honest inventory is the first step toward an actionable housing program.

3. Conversion When It Works; Reconstruction When It Does Not

Office-to-residential conversion can preserve usable structures, reduce some construction waste, and sometimes shorten delivery schedules. Hotels and motels may be especially promising because their rooms already contain plumbing and bathrooms. Vacant schools may accommodate housing plus shared community space. But not every office tower can become a good apartment building at a reasonable cost.

A responsible assessment must consider daylight and window access, floor-plate depth, stairs and fire exits, accessibility, ventilation, water and sewer capacity, asbestos or lead abatement, structural loading, and the layout of kitchens and bathrooms. Sometimes conversion is the economical choice. Sometimes removing the old building and constructing new homes yields better housing and more units. Sometimes the sensible answer is a combination: preserve one portion, rebuild another, and add homes where a parking lot once stood.

The goal is not to defend a technique. The goal is to deliver safe, healthy, lasting, genuinely affordable homes at a cost communities can sustain.

4. Housing Is More Than a Roof

A housing development should not be measured only by the number of keys it produces. People also need groceries, transportation, clinics, childcare, schools, places to gather, reliable internet access, and a sense of safety and belonging. A carefully designed redevelopment can put many of these essentials within walking distance.

Imagine a former commercial building becoming a community of apartments with a ground-floor clinic, childcare space, a public library branch, a shared garden, laundry facilities, a neighborhood kitchen, and rooms for job training. Its residents would not simply be housed; they would be part of a functioning community. This connects naturally with Terra Nova Development’s vision of Give and Take Community Centers—places where neighbors can share useful goods, access assistance, and support one another.

5. How Could We Pay for It?

There is no single formula that will work in every market. The 100 Gold Street proposal illustrates one possibility: cross-subsidy, in which some units or commercial uses generate revenue that helps support affordable housing. Elsewhere, a practical financing toolbox might combine:

  • Reuse or long-term lease of publicly owned land at terms tied to permanent affordability.
    • Low-cost financing through housing bonds, revolving loan funds, mission-driven lenders, or public housing authorities.
      • Federal, state, and local housing programs where eligible, combined with accountable private investment.
        • Mixed-income developments in stronger markets, where higher-rent homes help finance lower-rent homes.
          • Community land trusts and nonprofit ownership models that preserve affordability beyond the initial construction period.
            • Targeted code modernization and predictable approvals without compromising safety, accessibility, or tenant protections.

            Crucially, “affordable” must be defined relative to real local incomes—not just marketed as a pleasant-sounding word. Residents should be able to understand who qualifies, how rents are calculated, how long restrictions last, and what happens when ownership changes. Long-term affordability should be enforceable, independently audited, and transparent.

            6. What Would a National Building-Reuse Initiative Look Like?

            Imagine every city and county undertaking a straightforward, repeatable process. First, identify 50 to 100 promising public and private sites. Second, evaluate them using a shared technical checklist. Third, publish expected costs, plausible unit counts, affordability options, and infrastructure constraints. Fourth, invite developers, housing nonprofits, architects, engineers, and neighborhood residents to propose workable solutions. Fifth, approve a first group of projects, measure the results, and refine the model before expanding.

            A national initiative could support local governments with standard design patterns, environmental remediation assistance, finance templates, transparent procurement, and a public dashboard showing proposed units, units started, units completed, actual costs, and long-term affordability. Instead of another collection of disconnected pilot projects, we could build a learning system: every completed redevelopment would make the next one easier.

            7. Build Faster, But Do Not Forget the People Already There

            “Move faster” must not become an excuse to disregard residents or workers who depend on an existing building. Redevelopment can displace tenants, small businesses, public services, or culturally important institutions. A good plan identifies these effects early and provides serious mitigation, relocation support, or a right to return when appropriate.

            Community participation is not a nuisance to be eliminated. It is a source of local knowledge that can improve the project. At the same time, procedural complexity should not stretch a clearly beneficial and carefully reviewed housing proposal into an indefinite wait. We can provide meaningful participation, enforce strong safety rules, and still create predictable, timely decisions.

            8. Affordable Housing Requires More Than a Single Project

            The housing crisis did not arise from one cause, so it will not disappear with one tool. Building reuse should complement new housing construction, housing vouchers, tenant protections, preservation of existing affordable homes, supportive housing, and policies that encourage housing near jobs and transportation. Some families need a modest rent reduction. Others need deeply subsidized housing or continuing support because of disability, age, or other circumstances. A 25-percent-affordable mixed-income development can be valuable without being sufficient for everyone.

            The right question is not whether adaptive reuse will solve every housing challenge. It will not. The right question is whether we can afford to ignore assets that might help us solve a meaningful part of the problem. We cannot.

            9. From Artificial Scarcity to Practical Abundance

            A housing shortage is real for the family trying to pay the rent. But public policy can intensify or relieve that shortage. If a neighborhood has usable buildings and land that remain idle while legal, financial, and institutional barriers prevent them from becoming homes, the scarcity is partly a design failure. It is a problem we can address.

            We have architects who know how to design good homes, engineers who know how to evaluate structures, construction workers who know how to build them, nonprofits who understand local needs, and public officials empowered to coordinate the effort. The missing ingredient is often not a new invention. It is the decision to treat housing as essential civic infrastructure and organize our resources accordingly.

            10. The Future We Could Choose

            Picture a city ten years from now. A former office complex has become a lively residential district. A closed motel provides safe transitional apartments. A vacant school has been rehabilitated into homes for older adults with a neighborhood health center on the ground floor. A parking lot near transit now holds hundreds of apartments. Families spend less time commuting, older residents can remain near their communities, and local businesses enjoy new customers.

            These outcomes will not happen merely because we admire an ambitious announcement. They require careful feasibility studies, financing, public oversight, construction, and years of commitment. But they are achievable kinds of work. They begin when we stop asking why a building is empty and start asking whom it could serve.

            The future of affordable housing is not only in what we have yet to construct. It is also in what we already own, what we have stopped using, and what we can imagine together.

            We have the buildings. We have the land. We have the knowledge. We have the workforce. Let us apply some common sense—and make homes a priority.

            There is no them. There is only us.
