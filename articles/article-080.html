Direct and Indirect Costs of the Current System vs. Medicare for All

1. Direct Costs of the Current System

The U.S. healthcare system imposes enormous direct financial burdens on employers, households, and the economy:

Employer premiums exceed $23,000 per family per year, with employers paying $15,000–$20,000 per employee before any work is performed.

Small businesses pay more and get less: deductibles at small firms average $5,074, 43% higher than large firms.

Administrative waste is extreme: Duke Medical Center employs 1,600 billing clerks vs. 7 at Toronto General under single-payer.

Physician billing costs: $655 per capita.

Hospital billing costs: $1,200 per capita.

Total system spending: $5.28 trillion annually (2024).

2. Indirect & Hidden Costs of the Current System

These costs never appear on a premium invoice — but they hit employers harder than the direct costs:

Presenteeism (workers at work but impaired due to untreated conditions) accounts for 60–70% of all productivity-related health costs, costing employers $4,000–$6,000 per employee per year.

Absenteeism costs U.S. employers $225.8 billion annually, driven largely by chronic conditions worsened by delayed care.

Stress-related illness adds $300 billion in employer losses each year.

Diabetes indirect costs alone: $106 billion annually.

Combined indirect burden: $5,500–$7,200 per employee per year, on top of premiums.

These costs exist because millions of workers skip care, delay treatment, and avoid medications due to deductibles, copays, and coverage gaps.

3. Inflationary Structure of the Current System

The current system is structurally inflationary:

Employer premiums have risen 342% since 1999, while wages rose only 119%.

Fragmented payers → zero price discipline → prices rise every year, faster than wages and inflation.

Every major actor (insurers, PBMs, hospital systems, pharma) has a financial incentive for higher prices, not lower ones.

This inflation suppresses wages, reduces consumer spending, and depresses economic growth.

4. Direct Costs Under Medicare for All

A Yale School of Public Health model shows:

MFA reduces total U.S. health spending from $5.28 trillion → $4.24 trillion, saving $1.04 trillion annually.

Breakdown of savings:

$286.3B — administrative simplification

$295.6B — aligning payments to Medicare rates

$377.5B — drug price negotiation

These savings come from eliminating waste — not cutting care.

5. Indirect Cost Reduction Under MFA

Because MFA eliminates financial barriers to care:

Workers seek care earlier.

Chronic conditions are managed proactively.

Presenteeism and absenteeism drop.

Stress-related illness declines.

Productivity rises.

Employers save thousands per worker in hidden costs.

This is the largest economic gain of MFA — and the least discussed.

6. The Deflationary Effect of MFA

MFA is the only system capable of producing actual price deflation in healthcare:

A single national payer = monopsony power.

Unified negotiation → lower prices.

Administrative simplification → lower overhead.

Drug price negotiation → lower pharmaceutical costs.

Downstream effects:

Real wages rise.

Consumer purchasing power expands.

Businesses face lower operating costs.

The entire economy benefits from reduced healthcare inflation.

The current system cannot produce deflation — its incentives run in the opposite direction.

Bottom Line

Current System Total Cost (Direct + Indirect): Approaching $10 trillion/year.

Medicare for All Total Cost: ~ $5 trillion/year.

The U.S. is paying double for a system that delivers worse outcomes, lower productivity, and permanent inflationary pressure.

Medicare for All is not just cheaper — it is economically liberating.
