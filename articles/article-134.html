Reimagining American Healthcare

Terra Nova Healthcare: A For-Profit, Vertically Integrated Alternative

Download Article #134 as Word document

What if the next great advance in American healthcare did not begin with another insurance policy, another billing system, or another government program? What if it began by redesigning healthcare itself as one coordinated system?

That is the idea behind Terra Nova Healthcare (TNHC): a proposed for-profit corporation that would integrate insurance administration, hospitals, clinics, physicians, pharmacies, diagnostic services, and long-term care into a single accountable organization. It would initially compete in the Medicare Advantage marketplace and expand only as its economics and clinical performance justified doing so.

This is a conceptual business model, not an existing operating company. Its purpose is to demonstrate how systems engineering, modern technology, and a different set of business incentives might make comprehensive healthcare more accessible while creating a financially sustainable enterprise.

The Problem: We Have a Healthcare Industry, but Not One Healthcare System

American healthcare is a maze of insurers, networks, providers, benefit designs, authorizations, invoices, and competing information systems. A patient may have coverage and still struggle to find an appointment, transfer medical records, understand a bill, or afford treatment.

Clinicians often work inside organizations whose administrative and financial boundaries have little to do with the patient’s medical needs. Tests are repeated, referrals are delayed, and paperwork consumes time that could otherwise be spent caring for people.

TNHC begins with a systems-engineering question: Why should the patient be responsible for navigating a system that the healthcare organization itself should know how to coordinate?

The Core Idea: The Insurer and the Provider Become One

Rather than merely contracting with a patchwork of unrelated providers, TNHC would seek to own and operate much of its delivery network. Its doctors, nurses, specialists, laboratories, pharmacies, hospitals, rehabilitation facilities, and supporting staff would work within a shared operating system, with outside contracting where appropriate or legally necessary.

This vertical integration would allow the company to coordinate appointments, referrals, diagnostics, medications, discharge planning, and follow-up care. A unified patient record, subject to strong privacy and cybersecurity protections, could reduce repeated data entry and missed information.

The goal is not to make care impersonal. It is to remove the organizational friction that prevents healthcare workers from practicing their professions and patients from receiving timely treatment.

Medicare Advantage as the Starting Point

Medicare Advantage offers a possible entry point because private organizations already compete to provide Medicare-covered benefits under federal contracts. TNHC could propose plans built around its own coordinated provider network, emphasizing preventive care, predictable access, and low out-of-pocket costs.

The company would seek to offer qualifying members no deductibles or copayments for covered in-network core services, with a zero additional plan premium where financially feasible. This would not automatically eliminate the standard Medicare Part B premium: most Medicare Advantage members must continue paying it, unless a specific approved benefit offsets some or all of that cost.

Medicare Advantage organizations must meet federal requirements governing benefits, networks, enrollment, emergency care, quality, marketing, and oversight. TNHC could not simply declare every service covered everywhere; plan documents, applicable law, and contractual terms would control. The ambition would be to exceed minimum requirements where sustainable, not to pretend that regulations or costs disappear.

A Complete Continuum of Care

The long-range TNHC vision includes primary care, specialist visits, hospitals, laboratory testing, diagnostic imaging, prescription drugs, rehabilitation, skilled nursing, preventive programs, and coordinated care for chronic illness.

Dental, vision, and hearing services would be central to the proposed design rather than treated as peripheral conveniences. The company could also evaluate long-term care and selected elective services, including dental implants or laser vision correction, as later-stage offerings when clinical appropriateness and financial capacity permit.

Not every service would be included from day one. Comprehensive long-term custodial care, for example, is not generally a standard Medicare Advantage entitlement. TNHC would need a lawful funding model, separate coverage arrangements, or supplemental products before promising it. Elective cosmetic procedures likewise would require a clearly funded benefit or separate purchase option.

The promise is a progressively integrated continuum of care, supported by honest benefit descriptions and measurable access standards.

How a For-Profit Company Could Serve the Public

Profit is not inherently incompatible with good healthcare. The crucial question is how profit is earned. If an organization becomes more efficient by preventing avoidable illness, eliminating redundant administration, coordinating treatment, and reducing waste, both patients and shareholders may benefit.

But vertical integration can also create dangerous incentives: restricting referrals, underpaying staff, delaying necessary treatment, or steering patients toward company-owned facilities even when another provider is better. TNHC would need explicit safeguards against these practices.

Its business model should be judged by clinical outcomes, timely access, patient satisfaction, fair compensation, appropriate utilization, independent quality audits, and transparent financial reporting—not merely by margins. Medical necessity decisions should be governed by clinical evidence, appeal rights, and independent oversight.

The company should earn the right to grow by delivering better care, not by denying it.

Technology as the Operating System

Centralized scheduling could help patients reach the right professional without repeatedly starting over. Shared records could support safer medication management and smoother transitions between clinics, hospitals, and home care. Predictive analytics could identify patients who may benefit from earlier outreach.

Artificial intelligence could assist with documentation, capacity planning, supply chains, and patient communications. It should not be treated as an infallible clinician or an automatic reason to deny coverage. Human professionals must retain accountability for consequential clinical decisions.

The practical payoff would be less duplicated work, fewer missed handoffs, and more time for direct patient care. Privacy, cybersecurity, interoperability, bias testing, and informed consent would be core design requirements.

A Better Workplace for Healthcare Professionals

TNHC would seek to employ physicians, nurses, therapists, technicians, and other healthcare workers at competitive compensation, with appropriate clinical autonomy and reasonable staffing standards.

Reducing fragmented billing and authorization work could give professionals more time with patients. Integrated training and career pathways could improve continuity and retention. None of these gains is automatic: a large employer can also become bureaucratic. Worker feedback, patient-safety reporting, and meaningful professional governance would be essential.

The objective is a healthcare organization in which clinical excellence and operational excellence reinforce one another.

Expansion Beyond Medicare Advantage

Once a regional Medicare Advantage model demonstrates strong outcomes and financial stability, TNHC could explore Medicaid managed-care contracts where state law and procurement allow, then employer-sponsored and individual-market offerings.

Later possibilities might include partnerships or competitive contracts involving public-sector healthcare, rehabilitation, and specialized services. Veterans Affairs and military healthcare are distinct federal systems; participation would require separate legal authority and procurement, not merely a decision by TNHC to absorb them.

Each expansion would have to account for local licensing, state insurance regulation, capital requirements, antitrust review, provider capacity, and the needs of patients who prefer or require out-of-network care.

A ten-year national ambition is a strategic vision, not a demonstrated forecast. A prudent plan would use milestones rather than assume majority national market share.

The Economics Must Be Proven, Not Assumed

Vertical integration offers plausible savings, but owning hospitals, recruiting clinicians, building information systems, and meeting insurance reserve requirements require enormous capital. A company can fail even with an attractive vision if it misprices risk or expands faster than its clinical infrastructure.

TNHC would need actuarial modeling, regional demand studies, medical-loss projections, capital budgets, and rigorous pilot testing. It would have to demonstrate that lower administrative expense and better coordination offset the cost of richer benefits and expanded facilities.

Claims that the model could cover everyone without increasing government spending are hypotheses to test. Medicare Advantage funding depends on federal payment rules, risk adjustment, quality programs, and enrollment; broader universal access would require additional policy and financial decisions.

Success would be measured by independently verified cost per member, clinical outcomes, avoidable hospitalizations, appointment availability, patient-reported experience, and financial solvency.

A Practical Development Roadmap

Phase 1 — Design and validation: establish the corporate and clinical governance structure, commission actuarial and regulatory analyses, identify an initial region, and build the financing plan.

Phase 2 — Regional pilot: secure required approvals and contracts, build or acquire a right-sized care network, recruit clinicians, and test integrated scheduling, records, pharmacy, and referral systems.

Phase 3 — Evidence and refinement: publish quality, access, cost, workforce, and patient-experience metrics; correct problems before expanding.

Phase 4 — Responsible growth: enter additional markets and lines of coverage only when clinical capacity, capital, and regulatory compliance support expansion.

Phase 5 — Broader benefits: evaluate dental, vision, hearing, long-term care, and elective offerings against transparent affordability and sustainability criteria.

The Larger Vision

Terra Nova Healthcare asks whether a healthcare enterprise can make money by making care easier to obtain, easier to coordinate, and less wasteful to deliver. It is a challenge to the assumption that profitability must come at the expense of patients.

The company envisioned here would be privately operated and accountable for its finances, but its purpose would be unmistakably public-facing: better health, fewer barriers, stronger working conditions, and more value for every dollar spent.

America does not lack medical talent, hospitals, technology, or money. It lacks sufficient coordination among the extraordinary resources it already possesses.

TNHC is an invitation to redesign the system rather than accept its fragmentation as inevitable. It is a business proposition, a systems-engineering challenge, and a testable hypothesis about what healthcare could become.

There is no them. There is only us. And healthcare should work for all of us.
