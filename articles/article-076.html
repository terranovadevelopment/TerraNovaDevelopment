Medical Debt Relief Plan

Medical debt in the United States is not a personal failure. It is the predictable result of a system that forces individuals to bear financial responsibility for biological events they did not choose. The Medical Debt Relief Plan corrects this structural failure by shifting repayment from individuals to the government, using a simple, fair, and economically stabilizing model.

This plan is not forgiveness. It is repayment — a transitional justice mechanism that repairs the damage of the old system while the new system is built.

1. Eligibility Rule: Medicare-for-All Standard

All medical debt that would not exist under Medicare for All is eligible for government repayment. Under Medicare for All, medically necessary care is covered with no premiums, no deductibles, no copays, and no surprise bills. Therefore, any debt arising from medically necessary care qualifies.

Eligible Debt Includes

Emergency room visits

Hospital stays

Surgeries

Childbirth and maternity care

Cancer treatment

Mental health and addiction treatment

Chronic disease management

Specialist visits

Diagnostic imaging and laboratory tests

ICU and critical care

Ambulance transport

Prescription drugs

Medical credit cards (CareCredit, etc.)

Medical debt in collections

Medical debt that has resulted in judgments or garnishments

If Medicare for All would have covered it, the government pays it. This is the cleanest eligibility rule in social policy — no income tests, no paperwork, no moral judgment.

2. Government Assumption of Debt

Once a debt is verified as medically necessary, the government assumes full responsibility for repayment. Individuals experience immediate relief:

Collection calls stop

Lawsuits stop

Garnishments stop

Interest stops

Credit damage stops

The debt is transferred from the individual to the government, and repayment begins under the standardized national schedule.

3. Zero-Percent Interest Reset

All medical debt repayment is reset to 0% interest. This eliminates:

Compounding

Ballooning balances

Penalty fees

Interest traps

Profit extraction from illness

Every dollar paid goes directly to principal. Zero-percent interest transforms repayment from a predatory mechanism into a public service.

4. Ten-Year Equal Monthly Installments

All medical debt assumed by the government is repaid to lenders in equal monthly installments over a 10-year period.

Why 10 Years?

Predictability: Lenders receive stable, scheduled payments.

System Stability: Hospitals and clinics maintain steady revenue.

Budgeting: Government can plan long-term expenditures.

Fairness: Every debt is treated equally, regardless of income or circumstance.

Immediate Relief: Individuals are freed from the burden instantly, even though repayment continues in the background.

The repayment timeline affects only the government and lenders — never the individuals.

5. Why This Is Not “Debt Cancellation”

The debt is not erased. It is paid — fully and systematically. This distinction matters:

Lenders receive what they are owed.

Healthcare providers remain financially stable.

Individuals are freed from unjust burdens.

The government corrects a structural failure rather than issuing a bailout.

This is a structural correction, not charity.

6. Economic and Social Impact

A. Household Stability

Families regain:

Purchasing power

Credit stability

Savings capacity

Access to housing

Access to employment

B. Healthcare System Stability

Providers receive predictable payments, reducing:

Uncompensated care

Administrative waste

Collection costs

Financial volatility

C. National Productivity

Removing medical debt increases:

Workforce participation

Entrepreneurship

Consumer spending

Long-term economic growth

D. Moral Legitimacy

Medical debt punishes people for being human. This plan ends that punishment.

7. International Context

Countries with universal healthcare — Germany, France, Norway, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, Canada, the UK — have better health outcomes, lower administrative costs, and virtually no medical bankruptcy, according to the proposal. The United States is an outlier because it ties healthcare to personal debt. This plan seeks to align the U.S. with global norms while building toward Medicare for All.

8. Transitional Justice Framework

This plan functions as a bridge: Until Medicare for All exists, the government will pay all medical debt that Medicare for All would have prevented. It repairs the damage of the old system while the new system is constructed.
