Medicare for All and the End of the Group-Home Dilemma

Article #044 · November 11, 2026

Download Word article (.docx)

For millions of Americans with intellectual and developmental disabilities, the path to adulthood is shaped less by personal preference and more by the limits of the care system surrounding them. Families are told there are “options,” yet the system consistently funnels people into group homes and day programs—settings that can restrict autonomy, isolate individuals from their communities, and consume substantial resources. The group-home dilemma is not the result of a lack of imagination or love. It reflects a fragmented and underfunded care structure that leaves many families with few viable alternatives.

A Medicare for All approach that explicitly guarantees comprehensive long-term services and supports could change this reality. By redesigning how supports are financed and delivered, it could reduce pressures that make group homes feel inevitable and open the door to genuine independence.

I. The Roots of the Group-Home Trap

The current system is built on scarcity. Home- and community-based services (HCBS) are often rationed through Medicaid waivers, which vary dramatically by state and can come with long waiting lists. Families may need to demonstrate an institutional level of care to qualify for supports. Even when approved, service hours and available providers can be constrained.

When individualized support is limited, group homes and congregate programs may become the practical default. Families seeking independent living with tailored assistance can find that affordable services and accessible housing are unavailable. Independence is treated as an exception rather than the norm.

II. Medicare for All Makes Support a Universal Right

In this proposed model, Medicare for All would make long-term services and supports a guaranteed benefit, rather than relying on state waiver caps or inconsistent eligibility pathways. Essential services would include:

• Personal care and assistance with daily living

• Behavioral and developmental supports

• In-home nursing and respite care

• Transportation and assistive technology

• Community integration and tenancy support

These would be core benefits without premiums, deductibles, or copays. This outcome would require the legislation to explicitly cover long-term supports; universal medical insurance alone would not automatically guarantee them.

III. Independence Becomes the Default Setting

When support follows the person rather than the facility, individuals can choose to live in their own homes with assistance matched to their goals. Families can participate without bearing the entire financial burden. Community-based providers can focus on quality and continuity rather than institutional placement. Group homes remain an option for those who freely prefer them, not a required destination.

IV. The Financial Burden on Families

Families may face substantial expenses for private support workers, day programs, transportation, respite, and specialized housing. Universal coverage of qualifying supports could reduce these out-of-pocket costs and make independent living a realistic choice. National standards and transparent rates could also help control costs, although actual savings would depend on program design, staffing, and funding.

V. Care Coordination Finally Works

Medical treatment, behavioral services, housing assistance, and daily supports are often handled by different agencies with different rules. Families become unpaid care coordinators. A unified funding and coordination framework could reduce this fragmentation, improve continuity, and help prevent crises. Effective coordination would still require cooperation with housing, education, and local service systems.

VI. Community Inclusion Becomes Real

Community inclusion requires more than a promise. A comprehensive system would support employment, transportation, social participation, peer connections, and skill development. These services should be shaped by the person's preferences, with meaningful choice and safeguards against coercion.

Conclusion: A System Designed for Freedom

The group-home dilemma is not inevitable. It is intensified by a system that too often funds placements more reliably than individual independence. A Medicare for All framework that includes robust, universally accessible long-term supports could help replace those incentives with choice, coordination, and autonomy.

The goal is straightforward: people with disabilities should have the resources to live where they choose, with the people they choose, in ways that reflect their own goals and identities. That is the promise of a system designed for freedom.
